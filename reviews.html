Clair Obscur: Expedition 33

By Priya Nair, Lead Critic  ·  April 24, 2025

In a world where the Paintress writes a number on her monolith each year — condemning everyone of that age to vanish in a flash of paint — Expedition 33 follows the last cohort old enough to march against her before they too are erased. It's one of the most devastating premises in modern gaming, and Sandfall Interactive executes it with the confidence of a studio three decades its senior.

The combat is a revelation. Borrowing FromSoftware's DNA for precision timing and routing it through a turn-based JRPG structure, every enemy encounter demands your full attention. Parrying, dodging, and landing input-perfect combos creates a rhythm that rewards mastery without punishing curiosity. The skill trees are deep without being overwhelming. Two dozen hours in, you're still discovering synergies.

A World Worth Dying For

The world of Lumière is achingly beautiful — a faded Belle Époque dream collapsing under grief and time. Sandfall's artists have created environments that feel genuinely hand-authored rather than procedurally sprawling: every vista has been composed, every ruin tells a story. The soundtrack by Lorien Testard and Yale Miller is among the finest in any game this generation.

What elevates Expedition 33 beyond its mechanical excellence is the writing. Gustave, Maelle, Lune, and Verso are real people making impossible choices, and the game never lets you forget what's at stake. The relationship dynamics evolve naturally rather than through companionship meters. I paused the game twice just to absorb what had happened — not from confusion, but from the specific weight of grief the game had just handed me.

The Verdict
Clair Obscur: Expedition 33 is Sandfall Interactive's first game and, immediately, one of the finest RPGs ever made. It is emotionally devastating, mechanically brilliant, and built with an artistic ambition that most developers spend careers approaching. Play it.

Reviewed on PS5. Code provided by Focus Entertainment. 58 hours played to full completion.

Doom: The Dark Ages

By Marcus Webb, Senior Editor  ·  May 15, 2025

id Software set an impossibly high bar with Doom Eternal. The Dark Ages exceeds it not by going faster, but by going deeper. The medieval-era pivot is immediately arresting — a world of grotesque flesh-cathedrals and cosmic horror rather than the sci-fi corridors we've spent four games learning to sprint through.

The Shield Saw mechanic alone represents a leap in first-person combat design. Blocking is now offensive: a perfectly timed parry sends projectiles back and staggers heavy enemies, while the sword lunge closes distance in ways that reward players who treat every arena as a positioning puzzle. The Slayer is slower here, and that's entirely intentional — Dark Ages asks you to think before you move, and the result is combat that feels both tactical and brutally physical.

Scale That Actually Delivers

The Atlan mech sequences have no right to be as good as they are. When you pilot the colossal mechanical titan through burning demonic armies — intercepting missiles with your fist, cleaving skyscraper-scale demons at the ankle — the shift in perspective is staggering. Many games promise epic scale and deliver a cutscene. id delivers playable spectacle.

The Verdict
Doom: The Dark Ages proves one of gaming's oldest franchises still has genuinely new ideas. The Shield Saw parry system is the most creative combat mechanic in years, and the mech sequences are an all-timer. A must-play.

Elden Ring: Shadow of the Erdtree

By Marcus Webb, Senior Editor  ·  June 21, 2024

Shadow of the Erdtree doesn't expand Elden Ring — it completes it. FromSoftware's DLC is 40 hours of the most densely designed content the studio has ever produced, and that is a sentence I never expected to write about an expansion.

The Land of Shadow is a mirror of the Lands Between — familiar in structure but alien in tone. Where the base game felt like a shattered, sun-bleached world in slow descent, the Shadow feels suffocated, overgrown with something ancient and wrong. Every zone carries a specific sense of dread. The Scadutree fragments that power the new progression system are scattered across the world with the same environmental storytelling mastery FromSoftware has refined over a decade.

Messmer and the Art of the Boss Fight

The Messmer the Impaler storyline is among FromSoftware's most emotionally coherent narratives, with an ending that reframes everything you thought you understood about the Elden Ring world. His boss encounter is the studio's greatest: perfectly telegraphed, learnable, devastating, and narratively resonant.

The Verdict
Shadow of the Erdtree is the best DLC ever made. Not the best FromSoftware DLC — the best expansion in the history of the medium. If you played Elden Ring, you are obligated to play this.
